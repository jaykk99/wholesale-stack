# Amanda's Wholesaling Training Guide

*Everything you need to know to find deals, talk to sellers, and get paid. No experience needed. Read this top to bottom once, then use it as a reference before every call.*

---

## 1. What Wholesaling Actually Is

Here is the simplest explanation:

You find a house someone wants to sell fast and cheap. You get them to sign a paper that says you have the right to buy it. Then you find another person who actually wants to buy the house, and you sell them your right to buy it for a higher price. You keep the difference. That difference is called your assignment fee, and that is how you get paid.

You never own the house. You never fix anything. You never take out a loan. You are the middleman who connects a motivated seller with a cash buyer, and you get paid for making the connection happen.

Example: A seller agrees to sell their house to you for $80,000. You find a buyer who will pay $95,000 for it. You assign your contract to that buyer. At closing, you walk away with $15,000. That is a wholesale deal.

---

## 2. How Money Is Made (All 8 Ways, Explained Simply)

There is more than one way to profit from a deal. Here is every way, in plain language:

**1. Wholesale Assignment (the main one).** You sign a contract with the seller, then sell that contract to a buyer for a fee. Fastest and simplest. Typical fee: $5,000 to $20,000 per deal.

**2. Double Close.** You actually buy the house from the seller, then immediately sell it to your buyer in a second closing on the same day. You do this when the contract cannot be assigned, or when you want to hide your fee from the seller. You need short term funding for a few hours, which lenders called transactional funders provide.

**3. Fix and Flip.** You buy the house yourself, fix it up, and sell it for full price. Bigger profit, bigger risk, takes months. This is for later, once you have cash saved up.

**4. Buy and Hold (rental).** You buy the house and rent it out. Every month the rent comes in, you pay the expenses, and you keep what is left. This builds long term wealth. A good rental pays you every single month for years.

**5. Seller Financing.** Instead of the buyer paying all cash, the seller acts like the bank. The buyer puts some money down and makes monthly payments to the seller with interest. You can earn a fee for setting this up, or you can be the one collecting payments.

**6. Lease Option.** You rent the house with the right to buy it later at a set price. Then you find someone else who wants to rent it with the right to buy it from you at a higher price. You profit three ways: the upfront option fee they pay you, the monthly rent difference, and the price difference when they buy.

**7. Novation.** This is like wholesaling but fancier. The seller gives you written permission to market their house and find a buyer. When you find one, the seller sells directly to that buyer and pays you a fee from the proceeds. Useful when the seller owes more than the house is worth in some situations, or when assignment is not allowed.

**8. Wholetail.** You get the house under contract, do a light cleanup (mow the lawn, haul junk, maybe paint), then list it on the regular market for a higher price than a wholesale deal but lower than a full flip. It is the middle ground between wholesaling and flipping.

For now, focus on number 1: wholesale assignment. It is the fastest way to your first paycheck. The others are good to know about so you can spot opportunities.

## 3. The Full Deal Process, Step by Step

Here is the whole journey of a deal, from start to finish:

**Step 1: Find a lead.** A lead is a property that might be a deal. You find them by calling owners, driving around looking for run down houses, checking online listings, or getting lists of owners who might want to sell (like landlords who live far away, or houses going into foreclosure). Every lead goes into the app.

**Step 2: Call the seller.** You call and have a friendly conversation. Your only goal on the first call is to find out: do they want to sell, what is the condition of the house, and what price do they have in mind. Be warm, listen more than you talk.

**Step 3: Visit the property.** If the call goes well, you go see the house in person. Walk through every room. Take photos. Notice what needs fixing. This is where you estimate repair costs (section 5 shows you how).

**Step 4: Make an offer.** Using the 70 percent rule (section 6), you calculate your offer and present it to the seller. Be honest about how you got your number.

**Step 5: Get the contract signed.** If they accept, you fill out a purchase agreement. The app has templates that auto fill. The seller signs. Now you legally control the deal.

**Step 6: Find a buyer.** You send the deal to your buyers list. Buyers are investors with cash who want to buy houses. The app matches buyers to each property automatically.

**Step 7: Assign the contract.** A buyer agrees to pay more than your contract price. You sign an assignment agreement. Your fee is the difference.

**Step 8: Close and get paid.** A title company handles the paperwork. On closing day, the buyer pays, the seller gets their money, and you get your assignment fee. This usually comes as a check or wire transfer.

That is the whole game. Find them, call them, visit, offer, contract, buyer, close, paid.

## 4. How to Talk to Sellers

This is the most important skill. Forget everything about being salesy. Here is the mindset:

**You are a problem solver, not a salesperson.** Most sellers you talk to have a problem: a house they cannot keep up with, bills piling up, a property they inherited and do not want, tenants driving them crazy. Your job is to listen, understand their problem, and offer a solution. If you genuinely help them, the deal follows.

**Rules for every conversation:**

* Smile when you talk, even on the phone. People can hear it.
* Use their name. It makes everything warmer.
* Ask questions and then actually listen. Do not interrupt.
* Repeat back what they say so they know you heard them. "So the roof has been leaking for two years and you are tired of dealing with it, is that right?"
* Never lie. If you do not know something, say so.
* Never pressure. Pressure kills trust. If they say no, thank them and move on.
* Never talk bad about their house. It is their home, even if it is falling apart.
* Take notes during every call. Write down names, situations, timelines, and prices.

**What NOT to say:**

* Do not say "I want to lowball you" or anything about getting a cheap price.
* Do not say you are a real estate agent if you are not one.
* Do not promise things you cannot do, like a specific closing date you are not sure about.
* Do not trash talk other buyers or investors.
* Do not argue. If they get upset, stay calm and kind.

**The golden rule:** Treat every seller like they are your own family member going through a hard time. Because many of them are going through a hard time.

## 5. How to Estimate Repairs (The Simple Walkthrough Method)

You do not need to be a contractor. Here is a simple method that works:

Walk through the house room by room with your phone camera. For each area, check these things and jot down a rough cost:

**Outside:** Roof (missing shingles or leaks: $5,000 to $10,000 for a full replacement), siding damage, broken windows, overgrown yard, cracked driveway, leaning fence.

**Kitchen:** Cabinets ($3,000 to $6,000 to replace), countertops ($1,500 to $3,000), appliances ($2,000 to $4,000 for a basic set), flooring, plumbing leaks under the sink.

**Bathrooms:** Toilet, tub or shower, vanity, tile, water damage on the floor or ceiling below. A full bathroom redo runs $5,000 to $10,000.

**Every room:** Flooring (carpet: $3 to $5 per square foot installed, laminate or vinyl: $4 to $7), paint ($1,500 to $3,000 for a whole house inside), doors, light fixtures, outlets and switches.

**Big systems:** Furnace ($4,000 to $7,000 to replace), water heater ($1,000 to $2,000), electrical panel ($1,500 to $3,000 if it needs upgrading), plumbing (look for water stains, musty smells, low water pressure).

**The shortcut method:** For a quick estimate, use these per square foot numbers based on condition:
* Light cosmetic (paint, carpet, minor fixes): $10 to $15 per square foot
* Medium rehab (kitchen and bath updates, some systems): $25 to $40 per square foot
* Heavy rehab (gutted, major systems): $50 to $75 per square foot

Example: A 1,200 square foot house needing a medium rehab: 1,200 x $30 = $36,000 estimated repairs.

**Important:** Always add 10 to 20 percent extra on top for surprises. Old houses always hide surprises. If your estimate is $30,000, budget $35,000.

**Take photos of everything.** Your buyers will want to see the condition, and photos protect you if there is ever a disagreement.

## 6. How to Make Offers (The 70 Percent Rule)

This is the math that keeps you from losing money. Memorize this formula:

**Maximum Offer = (After Repair Value x 0.70) - Repair Costs**

**After Repair Value (ARV)** is what the house would sell for if it were fully fixed up. You find this by looking at similar houses nearby that sold recently in good condition. Those are called comps. The app can help, or ask Jay.

**Why 70 percent?** Because your buyer needs room to profit too. They will spend money on repairs, pay closing costs, pay interest if they borrowed money, and pay an agent when they sell. If they cannot make at least a 20 to 30 percent profit, they will not buy your deal. The 70 percent rule leaves enough meat on the bone for everyone.

**Example:**
* ARV (fixed up value): $200,000
* Repair costs: $40,000
* Maximum offer: ($200,000 x 0.70) - $40,000 = $140,000 - $40,000 = $100,000

So you offer around $100,000. If the seller accepts at $100,000 and your buyer pays $115,000, you make $15,000.

**How to present your offer to the seller:**

Be honest and walk them through it. Say something like:

"I looked at what fixed up houses sell for on your street, which is about $200,000. The house needs about $40,000 in work to get there. My buyers need to make a profit for taking on that risk, so the most I can offer is $100,000 cash, as is, closing whenever works for you."

Most sellers respect honesty about the math way more than a mysterious low number with no explanation.

**Your assignment fee** is the gap between your contract price and what the buyer pays. Aim for at least $5,000 per deal. Bigger spreads mean bigger fees, but do not get greedy on your first deals. Getting the first one closed builds your confidence and your buyers list.

## 7. How to Handle Objections (The 10 Most Common)

An objection is not a no. It is a question hiding inside a complaint. Stay calm, acknowledge what they said, and respond. Here are the 10 you will hear most:

**1. "Your offer is too low."**
"I hear you. Can I ask what number you were hoping for? [Listen.] Okay. Here is the thing: if you listed with an agent at that price, you would pay about 6 percent in commissions, plus closing costs, plus months of mortgage payments and insurance while you wait. When you subtract all of that, what would you actually walk away with? My offer is cash, no fees, no repairs, no waiting. A lot of sellers find the final number is closer than they thought."

**2. "I need to think about it."**
"Of course, this is a big decision. Can I ask what specifically you need to think through? Is it the price, the timing, or something else? If I can answer your questions now, would that help?"

**3. "I need to talk to my spouse / family first."**
"Absolutely, you should both be comfortable. What usually works best is if we can all talk together for five minutes so I can explain the numbers once and answer everyone's questions. Would tonight or tomorrow work for a quick call with both of you?"

**4. "I am not sure I want to sell."**
"That is totally fine. On a scale of 1 to 10, how serious are you about selling in the next few months? [If 6 or higher:] What would need to happen to make that an 8 or 9? [If 5 or lower:] I appreciate your honesty. Can I check back with you in a couple months in case things change?"

**5. "How did you get my number?"**
"Great question. I work with a service that finds property owners in the area who might be interested in selling. I am not on any do not call list service issue here, I just reach out to owners directly. I am happy to take you off my list if you would like."

**6. "Are you a real estate agent?"**
"No, I am not an agent. I am a local home buyer. I buy houses directly with cash, as is, no commissions or fees. That is different from listing with an agent, which takes longer and costs you commission."

**7. "I already have someone else interested / another offer."**
"That is great, I am glad you have options. Can I ask what they offered? [Listen.] I would love the chance to earn your business too. Let me take a quick look at the property and I will give you my best number within 24 hours. Then you can pick whichever is better for you. Fair?"

**8. "The house is worth more than that."**
"You might be right if it were fully fixed up. What I am looking at is what it is worth right now, as it sits, needing the work it needs. Have you gotten any other cash offers on it as is? That tells us what the market actually says today."

**9. "I need more time / the timing is bad."**
"No rush at all. When do you picture this happening? [Get a month.] Okay, I will check back with you two weeks before that. That way it is off your mind until then, and when you are ready we can move fast. In the meantime, is there anything about the property I should know so I can come back with a real number?"

**10. "This sounds like a scam."**
"I understand why you would be careful, and honestly that tells me you are smart about this. Here is how you can protect yourself: everything goes through a licensed title company, which is a neutral third party that handles the money. You do not pay me anything, ever. I get paid by my buyer at closing. You are welcome to have a lawyer or anyone you trust look at the paperwork before you sign anything."

**The pattern for every objection:** Acknowledge it, ask a question, listen, then respond to the real concern underneath. Never argue. Never get defensive.

## 8. How to Close

Closing means getting the seller to say yes and sign the contract. Here is how:

**When to ask:** After you have presented the offer and answered their questions, there will be a pause. That pause is your signal. Do not fill it with more talking. Ask directly: "Does that work for you?" Then wait. Silence is your friend here. The first person to talk loses.

**Closing techniques that work:**

* **The direct ask:** "Should we move forward with this?" Simple, respectful, effective.
* **The choice close:** "Would you prefer to close in two weeks or four weeks?" This assumes yes and lets them pick the detail.
* **The summary close:** "So just to recap: $95,000 cash, as is, no fees, I cover closing costs, and we close on your timeline. Does that sound right?" Then wait.
* **The takeaway:** "I want to be upfront: I can only hold this offer for a few days because I have other properties I am looking at. I would love for this one to work out." Only use this if it is true.

**After they say yes:**

1. Fill out the purchase agreement right away. Do not wait. Deals die with time.
2. Get it signed the same day if possible. Email, in person, whatever is fastest.
3. Send a copy to the title company to open escrow.
4. Thank them sincerely. "Thank you for trusting me with this. I will take good care of it."

**Following up:** Most deals do not close on the first call. The fortune is in the follow up. Here is the schedule:

* After first contact: follow up in 3 days
* Then: once a week for a month
* Then: once a month until they sell or tell you to stop

Every follow up is simple: "Hi [name], it is Amanda. Just checking in on the house at [address]. Has anything changed on your end?" That is it. Be the person who keeps showing up kindly. When they are ready, they will call you first.

**Track everything in the app.** Log every call, every note, every follow up date. Your memory will fail you. The app will not.

## 9. How to Work With Buyers

Buyers are investors with cash who buy the deals you find. Without buyers, you have contracts you cannot sell. Build your buyers list from day one.

**Where to find buyers:**

* The app has a Buyers tab. Add every investor you meet.
* Go to local real estate investor meetups. In Winnipeg, search Facebook for real estate investor groups.
* Look for "we buy houses" signs and ads. Those are buyers. Call them and ask what they are looking for.
* Check who is buying houses for cash in your area. Recent cash sales are public record.
* Ask every buyer: "What areas do you buy in? What price range? What type of property?" Write it all down.

**Presenting a deal to buyers:**

When you have a property under contract, send your buyers a simple deal package:

* Address and photos
* Your asking price (contract price plus your fee)
* Estimated ARV and repair costs
* Why it is a good deal (the numbers)

Keep it short. Investors are busy. Lead with the numbers.

**Example text to a buyer:** "Hi [name], I have a 3 bed 1 bath at [address]. Asking $115,000. ARV around $200,000, needs about $40,000 in work. Photos attached. Let me know if you want to walk it this week."

**Rules for working with buyers:**

* Never lie about the condition or the numbers. One lie kills your reputation forever.
* First come, first served is fine, but prioritize buyers who have actually closed with you before.
* Get proof of funds before you take a property off the market for someone. A serious buyer can show a bank statement.
* Always use a non refundable earnest deposit from the buyer when they commit. This keeps them serious.

## 10. Legal Basics and What to Avoid

This is not legal advice. Talk to a real estate lawyer in Manitoba before you do your first deal. But here are the basics everyone should know:

**What is legal:** Assigning a contract you signed is legal in most places, including Manitoba, as long as your contract has an assignment clause and you are transparent about what you are doing.

**What to avoid:**

* **Do not act like an agent.** Do not list other people's houses. Do not negotiate on behalf of someone else for a commission. You are a buyer who assigns contracts, not an agent.
* **Do not market a house you do not have under contract.** You can market your contract, but do not advertise the property itself like you own it unless you do.
* **Be transparent.** Tell the seller you are an investor who may assign the contract to another buyer. Put it in writing in the contract. Surprises at closing kill deals and reputations.
* **Use proper contracts.** The app has templates, but have a local lawyer review them once. It is worth the few hundred dollars for peace of mind.
* **Earnest money.** When you sign with a seller, you usually put down a small deposit ($100 to $1,000) to show you are serious. Make sure your contract says it is refundable during your inspection period.
* **Do not sign contracts you cannot perform on.** If you cannot find a buyer, you need a legal way out. That is what the inspection period clause is for. Never skip it.

**In Manitoba specifically:** Real estate rules can be different from the US advice you see online. The core concepts are the same, but get a Manitoba real estate lawyer to review your contract template before your first deal. One consultation is enough to get you started safely.

## 11. Daily Routine (What to Do Each Day to Get Deals)

Deals come from consistent daily action, not from one big effort. Here is your daily routine:

**Morning (1 to 2 hours):**

* Review your follow ups in the app. Who are you supposed to call back today? Call them first.
* Make 20 to 30 calls to new leads. Use the cold call script. Log every call.
* Check for new leads: any new listings, any responses to your marketing.

**Midday:**

* Visit any properties you have appointments for. Take photos, estimate repairs.
* Run your numbers on any property you visited. Make offers within 24 hours of visiting. Speed matters.

**Afternoon (1 hour):**

* Follow up with buyers on your active deals. Send new deals to your buyers list.
* Add any new buyers you met to the app.
* Update all your lead notes so nothing falls through the cracks.

**Weekly:**

* Review your pipeline: how many leads, how many offers out, how many under contract.
* Drive through one target neighborhood looking for distressed houses (driving for dollars).
* Attend one networking event or investor meetup if available.

**The numbers game:** Out of every 100 calls, you might talk to 20 sellers, get 5 who are interested, make 3 offers, and close 1 deal. That is normal. Do not get discouraged by no. Every no gets you closer to a yes. If you do the daily routine, the deals will come.

## 12. Glossary (Every Term You Will Hear)

**ARV (After Repair Value):** What the house would sell for fully fixed up. The most important number in every deal.

**Assignment:** Selling your right to buy a house (your contract) to another buyer for a fee.

**Assignment fee:** The money you make on a wholesale deal. The difference between your contract price and what the buyer pays you.

**Cash buyer:** An investor who buys houses with cash, no bank loan. These are your customers.

**Closing:** The final meeting where paperwork is signed, money changes hands, and the deal is done.

**Closing costs:** Fees for the title company, taxes, and paperwork. Usually 1 to 3 percent of the price.

**Comps (comparables):** Recently sold houses similar to yours, used to figure out what a house is worth.

**Contract:** The written agreement between you and the seller. Once signed, you control the deal.

**Double close:** Buying the house and immediately reselling it in two back to back closings.

**Earnest money (deposit):** A small amount of money you put down with the contract to show you are serious. Usually refundable during the inspection period.

**Equity:** The difference between what a house is worth and what is owed on it. High equity means the seller has room to sell at a discount.

**Escrow:** A neutral third party (usually the title company) that holds the money and paperwork during the closing process.

**Exit strategy:** How you plan to make money on a deal. Wholesaling, flipping, renting, etc.

**Inspection period:** A number of days after signing where you can back out of the contract for any reason. Usually 7 to 14 days. Always include this.

**Lead:** A property or seller that might turn into a deal.

**MAO (Maximum Allowable Offer):** The highest price you can pay and still make the deal work. Calculated with the 70 percent rule.

**Motivated seller:** Someone who needs to sell fast, usually because of a problem: financial trouble, divorce, inheritance, bad tenants, foreclosure, or a house in bad shape.

**Novation:** An agreement where the seller lets you find a buyer for their house and pays you a fee at closing.

**Pre-foreclosure:** The period after a homeowner falls behind on mortgage payments but before the bank takes the house. These sellers are often very motivated.

**Probate:** The legal process after someone passes away where their property gets transferred to heirs. Heirs often want to sell quickly.

**Proof of funds:** A bank statement or letter showing a buyer actually has the cash to buy.

**Rehab:** Fixing up a house. Short for rehabilitation.

**Seller financing:** When the seller acts as the bank and lets the buyer pay over time with interest.

**Skip tracing:** Finding someone's phone number and contact info when you only have their name or address.

**Title company:** The neutral company that handles the closing paperwork, checks that the title is clean, and distributes the money.

**Wholesaling:** Finding discounted houses, getting them under contract, and assigning the contract to a cash buyer for a fee.

**70 percent rule:** Never pay more than 70 percent of the ARV minus repairs. The golden rule of wholesaling.
